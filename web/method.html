Arguing with the data

Every number on the data page is the answer to a question that had a second, easier answer. This page is the argument: what the obvious move would have been, what it would have cost, and what the pipeline does instead.

None of the thresholds below were guessed. Each one was measured on the full history first and is recorded next to the run it governed, so a figure published a year ago can still be read against the rule that produced it.

Six arguments

each settled by measurement, not preference

The source is a public dump of what taxi meters and payment terminals reported. It contains rides billed at 276,423 miles, rides that end before they start, and fares below zero. None of that is a reason to distrust the dataset — it is a reason to say out loud what was done about it.

A broken value — is the whole trip garbage?

The easy move

Drop every row that breaks a rule. One filter, a table where every column is clean.

What it costs

$2.32M of fares and 88,743 trips thrown away, and the mean distance moves by 0.1 % — 3.166 mi instead of 3.169. Real revenue paid for a rounding difference.

What we do

The receipt is a fact; the measurements are attributes of it. A bad value is nulled and left out of its own average. The row and its money stay.

A row is quarantined only when no positive spend follows from it — no receipt, no trip. That is 1.83 % of January 2025, and its volume is reported separately as refunds_usd rather than silently deleted.

Which average is the honest one?

The easy move

Publish the mean. It is what everyone asks for, and it is one AVG() away.

What it costs

122 rows out of 3,475,226 — 0.0035 % — carry 47 % of all miles driven. The longest single "trip" is 276,423 miles; the circumference of the Earth is 24,901. The naive mean overshoots by 89 %.

What we do

Both ship, side by side. The median (1.67 mi) does not move when the tail is absurd; the mean does, and seeing them disagree is the point.

The map and the zone table on the data page therefore never show a mean without its median or its denominator within reach.

Is 165 miles a long trip or a broken odometer?

The easy move

Cap distance by magnitude: anything over 200 miles is not a taxi ride.

What it costs

Both errors at once. It missed 136 broken trips under the cap — 165.91 mi in 11.4 minutes for $17.70 — and nulled two genuine long hauls of 206 and 225 mi at 53–59 mph, billed $220 and $400 under rate code 5.

What we do

Judge distance by its ratio to duration, not its size. Magnitude stays only as a backstop for trips under a minute, where the ratio means nothing.

Which field is broken follows from the money: on 93 % of rows implying over 100 mph, the fare matches the duration and not the distance — median $14.46 against the $301 the odometer would imply. So the odometer is nulled and the timestamps are kept. 118 of the 122 trips over 200 mi break the speed rule anyway, so nothing was lost by dropping the magnitude cap to a backstop.

One coverage number, or one per row?

The easy move

Measure completeness once, put "distance is 97.5 % complete" in a footnote, move on.

What it costs

The footnote is true globally and useless locally. Newark Airport on 3 Jan 2025 has 28 trips and exactly one usable distance measurement. Its "average" is one number wearing a plural.

What we do

Every average carries its own denominator — distance_obs, duration_obs, fare_obs — per zone and per day. "Measured on how many trips" is a column, not a caveat.

Distance and duration have different denominators, so both are published. The map's speed metric stands on both at once and is only as trustworthy as the smaller one.

Should the timestamps be converted to UTC?

The easy move

Naive timestamps are a smell. Localise them, store UTC, sort it out at read time.

What it costs

They are already New York local time. Converting would shift every late-night trip into the wrong day — and nothing in the pipeline would have caught it, because the totals stay identical.

What we do

Treat them as naive local time, which is what they are, and partition on the local pickup date.

The evidence is the clock change: on 9 Mar 2025 between 02:00 and 03:00 the data holds zero trips, while an ordinary Sunday holds 4,809 in the same hour. Admitted limitation: trip durations spanning that hour are off by ±1 h.

Does this say what New Yorkers spend on taxis?

The easy move

Call the table taxi_trips, call the column revenue, let the reader assume.

What it costs

Yellow medallion cabs are 12.4 % of what the city spends on for-hire rides, and under 1.5 % outside Manhattan. Asked "what was spent on taxis in the Bronx", this output answers with a number 122× too small.

What we do

Put the limit in the names. The table is yellow_taxi_trips_by_zone, the column is yellow_revenue_usd, and the map says so under its title.

Measured against HVFHV, Green and FHV for January 2025. The number is not wrong; only a name that invites reading it as the whole market would be.

How we work

measure · encode · record

A threshold nobody measured is a number somebody liked. Every rule below started as a query over the full history, became an expression, and ends up written into the manifest of every run it governed — so the rule can change without making last year's output unreadable.

measure Query the whole history for the shape of the problem: how many rows, how much money, which field is actually the broken one.
──▶
encode One declarative rule: an expression that is true when violated, and an action — quarantine the row, or null one field of it.
──▶
record The run manifest stores the counts and the thresholds that applied, next to the output they produced.

Two severities, not one

A missing column and an implausible value are different kinds of event and get different answers.

Where a refused row goes

Nothing is deleted. In a regulated setting the answer to "which rows fell out" has to be a query, not a memory.

The rules, and what they have caught

Rules are counted independently — one row can break several at once — so these do not add up to the quarantine count, and the numbers do not depend on the order the rules run in. Two of the eight refuse the row; the other six refuse one field and keep the trip.

What the run is judged against

Gates, not rules: these do not clean anything, they stop a bad month from being published at all.

The ±40 % volume gate comes from 29 measured months: the largest genuine jump was +21.9 % (2024-09) and the largest drop −13.5 % (2026-01). A gate breach fails the run immediately rather than retrying — the retry would download the same bytes and reach the same verdict.

What this still gets wrong

known, measured, unfixed

The list a reader would otherwise have to reconstruct from the code. Each of these is a decision, not an oversight — but they are limits of the published numbers all the same.

LimitWhy it is there
Durations across the DST change are off by ±1 h The source timestamps carry no offset. Two hours a year are ambiguous and no reconstruction would be honest.
A quarantined row loses its trip count A refund is not a ride, so it leaves trips. Its volume is published separately as refunds_usd instead of vanishing.
Restatements are reported, not gated Nobody has measured how much a recomputed month may legitimately move. A threshold here would be the one invented number in the system.
passenger_count is not published NULL on 15.5 % of rows under Flex Fare. Rules protect the published metrics; dropping 540k trips over a column nobody publishes would be the actual error.
The map's median is a median of daily medians Medians do not sum across partitions. Means are re-derived from weighted numerators and denominators and are exact; the median over the whole history is an approximation, and the page says so where it is shown.
Raw bytes are overwritten, not versioned Lineage works by reference — ETag and sha256 in the manifest. Old raw versions expire after 7 days: insurance against a bug, not an archive. The source can be fetched again.
Zone attribution is pickup only One row is one (pickup day, pickup zone). That is where demand appeared; a trip to JFK counts in Midtown, not at the airport.

The numbers themselves — 263 pickup zones on a map, eight metrics, daily series per month, and the mean sitting next to the median it should be read against.
